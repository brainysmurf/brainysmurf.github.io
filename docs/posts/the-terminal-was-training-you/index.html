The terminal was training you all along

#ai #education #leadership #tools

There’s a black screen. A blinking cursor. Nothing is happening, and nothing will happen, until you decide what you want.

I’ve been thinking about the terminal lately — as you might be able to tell it the core visual motif for my website. Partly, it’s nostalgia — I came up through open source, and the command line was where I learned to think. Headphones on, terminal up. But I’m thinking of it partly because something is happening that I didn’t expect: the terminal’s vocabulary is crossing over into mainstream conversation. Specifically, the word grepping.

grep is a Unix command from 1974. You give it a pattern and a body of text; it returns the matches. Find the signal, discard the rest. It never made it into common parlance because there was no reason for most people to think in those terms.

Now there is. When people describe how AI models retrieve information during a task — scanning documents, pulling relevant context, filtering through a codebase — they’re reaching for the most accurate word. And that word is “grepping.” Not because they learned the terminal. Because the terminal’s concepts were always the right ones.

The tool didn’t change. What changed is what can use it.

Here’s the thing about software: it’s built in layers, and the old layers don’t disappear. They get recruited.

When an AI tool like Claude Code searches a codebase, there is almost certainly a binary that does exactly what grep does. It’s a “primitive” from 50-year-old tech. What’s new is what’s above it — a language model that can manipulate it.

The tool itself was always capable of being utilized in this way. It just needed more context before pressing enter.

That’s the thing the terminal always required.

The cursor blinks. It waits. It will not meet you halfway, suggest an alternative, or interpret your vague impulse charitably. You have to know what you want before anything happens.

That turns out to be harder than it sounds. Humans are naturally imprecise about what they want — we say “improve parent communication” when we haven’t yet decided what that means, let alone how to measure it. Schools have a framework for exactly this: SMART goals. Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. The whole point is to force a vague intention into something formed enough to act on. The terminal enforced the same discipline, just mechanically, via syntax errors instead of missed follow-through.

Specificity isn’t pedantry. It’s the difference between intention and action.

Claude Code, a tool with a desktop analog but best used when living in the terminal, has shifted the demand. You no longer need syntactic precision — natural language works fine. But the quality requirement hasn’t gone away. It’s just moved upstream, and become harder to see. The machine won’t crash on a vague instruction. It’ll produce something adjacent to what you meant, and you’ll spend time wondering why it didn’t quite land.

What does this mean for schools?

The gap in AI outcomes your staff are experiencing isn’t a technology literacy gap. It’s an intention gap.

The people getting the most out of AI tools aren’t necessarily the most technical. They’re the ones who arrive with a complete thought. Who have done the work — before they open the tool — of knowing what they actually want, what counts as done, and what the constraints are.

The terminal trained that habit for 50 years, in a population small enough to be invisible. Everyone else is learning it now, through results that are almost-but-not-quite right.

Schools have long struggled to attract and retain technical staff. That’s not new, and it’s not going away. But the terminal’s resurgence through AI tools opens a different path: training the professionals you already have. The intention gap is closeable. It doesn’t require a computer science background — it requires the same discipline SMART goals were always asking for, applied to a new kind of tool.

The word grepping didn’t make it into the mainstream through computer science classes. It’s arriving now because the concept underneath it — find the relevant thing, bring it in, act on it — turned out to be universal. It was always going to be needed. It just waited, like the cursor, for something powerful enough to put it to use.

The cursor is still blinking. Your move.
