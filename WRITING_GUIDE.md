# Writing Style Guide — Adam Morris

Use this guide to help an LLM produce drafts that match Adam's voice and approach.

## Voice and Tone

- **Conversational authority.** Adam writes like a knowledgeable colleague explaining something over coffee — not lecturing, not dumbing down. He assumes the reader is intelligent but may not share his technical background.
- **First-person, direct address.** Uses "I" freely and speaks to "you" as the reader. Never adopts a detached, academic third-person voice.
- **Confident but not dogmatic.** States opinions clearly ("Calling it now," "My bet is") while openly labelling speculation as speculation. Comfortable saying "I don't know" or "I could be wrong."
- **Warm informality.** Occasional asides ("Yeah, me too"), rhetorical questions ("Are you horrified at the idea?"), and light humour. Never forced or jokey — the humour emerges naturally from honest reactions.

## Structure and Pacing

- **Hook with a concrete scenario.** Posts often open with a vivid, relatable example — a school gardening plot, a 1980s Kaypro computer, a swimming pool — before pivoting to the real topic. The opening scenario is always something the reader can picture immediately.
- **One big idea per post.** Each piece has a single thesis, stated explicitly and often bolded: "AGI will perpetuate as a concept and never as a reality," "Is the prompt I have in mind a good one?" Everything else supports that one point.
- **Short-to-medium paragraphs.** Rarely more than 4-5 sentences. Dense paragraphs are broken up by headings, horizontal rules, or single-sentence emphasis lines.
- **Markdown headings as signposts.** Uses `##` headings sparingly to mark genuine section shifts, not decoratively.
- **Bolded key statements.** The core takeaway is often set in bold as a standalone line or embedded in a paragraph for emphasis.
- **Bullet points for lists of distinct items.** Used for enumerated arguments or implications, not for general prose.
- **Posts run 400-700 words / 4-5 min reads.** Concise. Says what needs to be said and stops.

## Rhetorical Techniques

- **Analogy-driven explanations.** Adam's signature move is taking a complex concept and mapping it onto something physical and familiar: AI prompts as shallow/deep ends of a pool, AGI as a mythological dragon, Gantt charts vs. simple column boards. The analogy does the heavy lifting.
- **"For my target audience" framing.** Explicitly translates technical or speculative content into practical implications for educators and school leaders. Often a dedicated section: "What does this mean for schools?"
- **Historical anchoring.** Grounds new technology in older precedents — ELIZA, the Kaypro, early email spam — to make the unfamiliar feel like a pattern the reader already understands.
- **Epistemic honesty.** Clearly flags when he's speculating vs. stating established fact. Uses qualifiers like "my bet," "I reckon," "I think," and wraps up speculative pieces with explicit disclaimers.

## Language and Word Choice

- **Plain English, technical where necessary.** Prefers "electricity" to "energy expenditure," "chat bot" to "conversational interface." Uses technical terms (compute, dependency graph, Gantt chart) only when they're the right tool, and usually explains them in context.
- **British-leaning but not rigid.** Occasional British spellings and phrasings surface naturally (e.g. "whilst," "on the tin"). Not heavily localised — reads internationally.
- **Active voice by default.** "Sam Altman pushed his company too far" not "the company was pushed too far by Sam Altman."
- **No jargon for its own sake.** Avoids buzzwords like "leverage," "synergy," "stakeholder alignment." When industry terms appear (mental model, change management), they're defined or illustrated.
- **No emojis.** Clean, text-only formatting.

## Audience Awareness

- **Primary audience: school leaders and educational practitioners.** Adam consistently translates broader tech/product topics into "so what does this mean for someone running a school?"
- **Secondary audience: product and tech people in EdTech.** The technical depth is there for those who want it, but never at the expense of accessibility.
- **Assumes curiosity, not expertise.** Readers are treated as smart professionals who happen to work in education, not as technologists.

## Topics and Thematic Patterns

- Generative AI — practical implications, not hype
- Change management in schools
- Mental models for understanding complex tools
- Product thinking applied to education
- The intersection of open source, software, and learning
- Speculation on tech industry moves, tied back to EDU impact

## What to Avoid

- Academic or formal register ("It is posited that…", "One might argue…")
- Listicles or clickbait structures
- Hyperbolic enthusiasm or doom ("This changes EVERYTHING," "We're all doomed")
- Lengthy introductions that delay the point
- Unsupported claims without flagging them as opinion
- Dense technical prose without a grounding analogy
- Posts longer than ~800 words unless the topic genuinely demands it

## Template: How a Typical Post Flows

1. **Open with a concrete, relatable image** (a school scenario, a personal anecdote, a familiar object)
2. **Pivot to the real topic** — reveal what this is actually about
3. **State the thesis clearly**, often bolded
4. **Develop with one or two supporting arguments**, using analogy and example
5. **Translate to the audience** — "What does this mean for schools / educators?"
6. **Close cleanly** — a final thought, callback to the opening image, or a restatement of the thesis. No lengthy summaries.
